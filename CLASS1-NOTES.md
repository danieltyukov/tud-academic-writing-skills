## Scientific Writing Principles

Priority order: **Precise > Clear > Brief**

Scientific text components:
- **Objective** - what are the goals?
- **Motive** - why is this important?
- **Method** - how was it done?
- **Results** - what was found?
- **Implications** - what does it mean?
- **Limitations** - what are the caveats?

---

## Literature Review: Key Concepts

### What It Is
- A survey of scholarly sources providing an overview of current knowledge
- Identifies relevant theories, methods, and **gaps** in existing research
- **NOT just a summary** - it's critical evaluation and synthesis

### Types
- **Explorative:** broad overview of a field/topic
- **Comparative:** focused comparison of approaches/methods/findings

### Synthesising vs Summarising (Critical Distinction)
- **Summarising:** restating what individual sources say (source-by-source)
- **Synthesising:** combining multiple sources to build an argument or identify patterns (theme-by-theme)
- The review MUST synthesise, not just summarise

## Paper Structure (IMRD)

### Introduction (200-300 words)
- **Background/Motivation:** context, why this topic matters
- **Objective:** research question or purpose statement, linked to the motive
- **Outline:** preview of paper structure (unless using strict IMRAD)

### Method (100-200 words)
- Search strategy: which databases, which keywords, Boolean operators
- Selection criteria: how sources were filtered
- Quality check: how source quality was assessed
- Must be **replicable**

### Results / Body (~1300-1400 words)
- Organised thematically (not source-by-source)
- Each section contributes to answering the research question
- Uses tables/figures to clarify findings
- Critical comparison of sources throughout

### Discussion / Conclusion (~200 words)
- Repeat aim and answer the research question
- Summarise key results
- Implications of findings
- Limitations of the review
- Recommendations for future research

## Research Question Guidelines

A good research question should:
- Be neither too broad nor too narrow
- Be clear (no ambiguous words)
- Not be too long
- Be ONE question only
- Start with a wh-word (what, why, when, who, where, how, to which extent)
- Cover everything discussed in the text
- Lead to an academic debate
- Be achievable (given available time/means)

## AI Use in Academic Writing

### Responsible AI Use - The 5 P's
1. **Prompt** - effective use starts with an effective prompt
2. **Proof** - critically evaluate AI output using your own thinking
3. **Privacy** - never feed personal/confidential information to AI tools
4. **Presentation** - maintain your own voice in the writing
5. **Property** - you are responsible for your work, even when using AI

### AI Tools Mentioned
**General:** Copilot, ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude
**Academic:** Elicit, SciSpace, Connected Papers, ResearchRabbit, Undermind, Litmaps

### Key Takeaway
- AI is a tool, not the holy grail
- Do not outsource the process to AI
- Be transparent about your use of AI (required acknowledgement statement)

## First Steps (Explore Phase)

1. **Brainstorm on your topic**
   - What interests you? What are hot topics in your field?
   - Use AI tools (Copilot, Perplexity) or traditional search engines
   - Ask for 5 possible research directions with descriptions

2. **Explore academic literature**
   - Use academic AI tools (Elicit, Perplexity) or Google Scholar
   - Evaluate: Are sources relevant and trustworthy?
   - Use snowballing to find more sources

3. **Develop research question**
   - Narrow down from brainstorming and literature exploration
   - Apply the research question guidelines above

### Further Searching
- Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science
- TULib (TU Delft Library) for database access
- Use both AI-powered and traditional search methods
