## What is a Literature Review?

A survey of scholarly sources on a specific topic that:
- Provides an overview of current knowledge
- Identifies relevant theories, methods, and **gaps** in existing research
- Analyses, synthesises, and critically evaluates sources
- Does NOT just summarize sources

**Main goal:** Identify scientific/knowledge gaps in a research area, leading to recommendations for future research.

## How to Write This Literature Review (Step-by-Step)

1. Form a trio with two other students
2. Choose a topic relevant to your field (or pick from starting articles list on Brightspace)
3. Perform literature search - minimum 9 academic sources (journal articles, conference proceedings, textbooks)
4. Introduce the topic and justify the choice (scientific and/or social relevance)
5. Compare literature analytically - how do authors differ, where do they overlap? Evaluate critically
6. Give meaning to the comparative analysis - explain differences/overlaps (institutional, cultural backgrounds?)
7. Conclude with clear picture of state of knowledge - what is accepted/debated state-of-the-art, what remains disputed/on agenda
8. Translate insights into recommendations for other scientists

## Writing Guide: Key Sections

### The Discussion
- Reflects on the outcomes of your study as presented in the results section
- Reference: Silyn-Roberts (2013), Chapter 2 (p. 38) and Chapter 6 (p. 98)
- See Academic Phrasebank (University of Manchester) for example sentences

### The Conclusion
The conclusion is one of the most important sections — many readers skip straight to it.

Write it as follows:
1. Write a **separate section** called "Conclusion" (or "Discussion & Conclusion")
2. Present a **clear answer** to your main question / relate conclusions to your main aim
3. **Evaluate the validity** of that answer in light of possible limitations
4. State the **contribution** of your research (scientific and societal importance)
5. Reflect on **possible applications** and suggest avenues for **future research**

Reference: Silyn-Roberts (2013), Chapter 2 (p. 39) and Chapter 6 (p. 101)

### The Abstract
Probably the **most important section** — readers use it to decide if the rest is relevant. Often written last, but should NOT be done hastily.

Must include: motivation, objective, methods, conclusion (see Required Document Elements above).

Reference: Silyn-Roberts (2013), Chapter 2 and Chapter 6 (p. 88)

### The Title
A title should:
1. Indicate the **topic** of the study
2. Indicate the **scope** (neither overstating nor understating significance)
3. Be **self-explanatory** to readers in the chosen area

Reference: Silyn-Roberts (2013), Chapter 2 (p. 19) and Chapter 6 (p. 87); Swales & Feak (2004)
