# Important Definitions in Argumentation

## Standpoints

A standpoint is a statement containing a claim or opinion. Three types:

1. **Descriptive (factual)**: describes a state of affairs that can be checked with factual information. E.g., "The summer of 2023 was the hottest summer ever."
2. **Evaluative (normative)**: conveys evaluation or judgement. E.g., "The Starry Night by Van Gogh is a beautiful painting."
3. **Prescriptive**: proposal for a course of action. E.g., "The Netherlands should invest in hydrogen energy."

A speaker/writer putting forward a standpoint takes on a **burden of proof** — must provide evidence.

## Arguments and Argumentation Structures

An **argument** (or premise) is a statement used to defend or attack a standpoint.

- **Single argumentation**: one argument supports a standpoint
- **Sub-arguments**: further evidence to support an argument (can be several layers deep)
- **Dependent arguments**: work together to support a standpoint. If one is refuted, the whole chain is refuted.
- **Independent arguments**: support a standpoint independently. If one is refuted, others are not affected. Together they make a stronger case.

Real-life argumentation often combines all three types.

## Criticizing Arguments

Two general types of criticism:

1. **Content**: Is the argument true?
2. **Justificatory force**: Is the connection between argument and standpoint strong? Does the reason justify the claim?

- To anticipate criticism on **content** → provide sub-arguments (further evidence)
- To anticipate criticism on **justificatory force** → provide additional arguments (dependent or independent)

## Argumentation Types (Schemes)

The sort of argumentation used to defend a standpoint. If used correctly = reasonable argument. If used incorrectly = fallacy.
